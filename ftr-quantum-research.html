Does quantum computing help you trade transmission congestion?

A Financial Transmission Right pays its holder the price difference between two points on the power grid — the standard hedge against congestion. This is a report on three linked studies built around that one instrument: forecasting what it will pay, choosing which ones to hold, and pricing an option on one, each using a quantum method benchmarked honestly against its best classical counterpart on real NYISO market data.

All three studies run on the same real data: twelve months of NYISO's public day-ahead price and load reports across its eleven real grid zones — nothing simulated. The pieces build on each other, too: the portfolio optimizer is wired to consume the forecasting model's actual output, not a separately-computed statistic standing in for it. Every result below is reported the way I'd want a hiring manager to report a research finding to me — stated plainly, checked against an independent classical reference, never rounded up toward "quantum wins" when the honest answer is a tie.

01 · forecasting

Forecasting the spread

Two quantum kernel methods, tuned through a real hyperparameter sweep, predict tomorrow's day-ahead congestion price between NYISO's NYC and West zones — benchmarked against a tuned classical kernel, gradient boosting, and an LSTM, all on the identical test set with bootstrap confidence intervals on every number.

In dollars per megawatt-hour. The quantum and classical kernel's confidence intervals almost completely overlap — not a loss, not a win, a genuine statistical tie.

What actually happened

The plain LSTM won outright, and the reason is architecture, not quantumness: it sees a full week of raw price history, where every kernel method — quantum or classical — only sees a five-number snapshot of it. That's a real, useful finding on its own: the bottleneck in this problem was how much context the model could see, not which kernel computed the similarity between points.

Tie

Quantum matches classical, but neither beats a plain LSTM with more context. Kernel choice wasn't the bottleneck here — architecture was.

02 · portfolio

Choosing which contracts to hold

A portfolio optimizer picks 10 of 110 real transmission contracts to hold at once, framed as a constrained optimization problem and solved with a quantum-inspired annealing method, then checked against an exact brute-force solution and a classical benchmark.

What the data itself turned up

The real finding wasn't about quantum versus classical. All 110 possible contracts are just price differences built from the same 11 underlying zone prices — so the whole universe only has about 11 truly independent sources of risk, not 110. The unconstrained mathematical optimum ends up concentrated in just two contracts, no matter how many positions you're allowed to hold. That's a genuinely different problem shape than a stock portfolio, where hundreds of names really are close to independent bets.

At small scale, where an exact answer is checkable, both the annealing method and a real gate-based quantum algorithm (QAOA) landed on the exact right answer. That's a correctness result: the quantum method works. It isn't a speed result — the classical annealing solved the identical problem faster with less machinery.

Wiring in a real forecast

The clean test of whether any of this matters in practice: swap one contract's assumed value for the forecasting model's actual, real-time prediction, and see whether the recommended portfolio changes.

The forecast disagreed with history on both counts that matter: lower expected value, higher risk, even measured over the identical window. Once that became the actual input, the optimizer dropped the contract entirely and picked a different one instead. Not a relabeling exercise — a forecast that, when actually used, changed the recommendation.

Structural finding

The domain shape mattered more than the algorithm, and using the model's real forecast changed the answer.

03 · pricing

Pricing an option on the spread

Quantum Amplitude Estimation prices a call and a put on the same NYC–West spread, centered on the forecasting model's own prediction rather than a separately-assumed number — and because a congestion spread can go negative (unlike a stock price), the underlying distribution had to be the one real markets already use for exactly that situation, not the standard textbook one.

At the tightest precision tested: over 4 billion classical samples vs. well under a million quantum queries for the same accuracy.

Why the gap is bigger than a stock option's

That gap is bigger than the equivalent result on a standard stock option, and the reason is itself informative: this is the same quadratic-versus-linear scaling law in both cases, just made more dramatic here because a real congestion spread is far noisier than a near-the-money stock option, and the classical method's cost scales directly with that noise.

Win

The clearest, largest measured speedup across this whole line of research — the theoretically-predicted advantage shows up cleanly on real, noisy market data.

What it means

Put together, these three studies say something more useful than "quantum computing works" or "quantum computing doesn't" — they say where it does, on a real problem, checked instead of assumed.

  • The advantage isn't uniform, and that's the finding. Pricing showed a real, large, theory-matching speedup. Forecasting showed a tie. Portfolio selection showed the algorithm working correctly but not mattering as much as a fact about the data itself. All three are reported as measured, not smoothed into one headline.
  • Moving from a textbook problem to a real one broke something every single time, on purpose. A percentage-error metric stopped making sense once prices could go negative. A risk-tolerance parameter tuned for stock returns let risk swamp return by roughly 100× on raw energy prices. A standard option-pricing distribution assumed positivity a real congestion spread doesn't have. Every one of these was caught by checking the output against a known-correct answer, not by reasoning about it in advance — that discipline is the actual point of doing the work this way.
  • The pieces are wired together, not just adjacent. The portfolio optimizer consumes the forecasting model's real output. That's a small integration in absolute terms, but it's the difference between three demos sitting next to each other and one system where a prediction can actually change a decision.
