Does a proven quantum speedup survive a harder option?

Quantum Amplitude Estimation (QAE) has a proven quadratic sample-efficiency advantage over classical Monte Carlo for pricing a vanilla option — I validated that on real IBM hardware in an earlier study. A swing option is structurally harder: instead of one payoff at one date, the holder gets several exercise dates coupled by a shared volume limit, so every decision depends on every other one. A literature search found zero prior work combining QAE with swing options — this is a study of whether that speedup actually survives the jump.

Two separate literature searches turned up published quantum work for vanilla options, multi-asset baskets, and Bermudan (multi-date) options — but nothing for swing options specifically. 01 · the contract

Why a swing option is a harder problem

Common in gas and power contracts: the holder gets several exercise dates, and at each one may take a unit at a fixed strike or skip — but the total taken across every date must stay within a floor and a ceiling.

That shared constraint is what makes it hard. A strip of independent options can be priced one date at a time. A swing option can't: whether it's worth taking a unit today depends on how many you've already taken and how many chances remain — which means the dates are coupled together, not separable. Toy scenario used here: 3 exercise dates, take between 1 and 2 units total, same market convention (S₀=2.0, volatility=40%, risk-free rate=5%, strike=1.896) as the earlier vanilla-option study, so the two are directly comparable.

QAE can't solve the optimization itself. Amplitude estimation extracts the expected value of a known function of a random variable — it doesn't do dynamic decision-making. So the actual approach is a split: a classical solver finds the optimal exercise policy first, then a quantum circuit evaluates the expected payoff of following that fixed policy, and QAE estimates that expectation.
02 · a real bug

Caught by disagreement, not inspection

The first version of the classical pricer had a real bug: it chose each date's exercise decision before that date's price was actually known, then applied the same decision no matter what price came in — quietly deleting the one thing that makes an option valuable.

The tell

It passed its own built-in check, because that check enumerated decision policies over the same wrong assumption about what information a policy could use — the bug and its test shared a blind spot. It only surfaced because a second, independently-built pricer (Monte Carlo simulation) consistently priced the option higher than the first one — which is backwards. A worse decision-making process can only produce a lower price than the true optimum, never a higher one. That contradiction was the tell.

After the fix

Moving the decision to after the price is observed — the correct information order — brought the fixed pricer to $0.1508 at the same precision, matching both independent references (Monte Carlo, and a from-scratch numerical integration with no shared code) to within a fraction of a percent. Every validation check in the rebuilt pricer now passes against exhaustive brute-force search, using the corrected assumption throughout.

Caught & fixed

The bug survived its own test suite. It didn't survive a second, independently-built method disagreeing with it.

03 · the circuit

Encoding a path-dependent payoff exactly

With the classical policy verified correct, the quantum circuit encodes every possible discretized price path's payoff under that fixed policy, then lets amplitude estimation extract the expected value.

At this toy scenario's resolution there are 64 possible discretized price paths. Rather than approximate, this version encodes all 64 exactly — one controlled rotation per path, each carrying that path's exact payoff under the verified policy. Exact, but expensive: unlike a vanilla option's payoff (a handful of comparator gates), a path-dependent, multi-decision payoff doesn't obviously reduce to a small circuit. That's the real open question this version deliberately sidesteps in favor of getting the answer right first.

At the tightest precision tested: ~20,000 classical samples vs. roughly 1.6 billion quantum gate-equivalents for equivalent accuracy — because each quantum query here costs far more than each classical sample.

What that actually means

The theoretical result — fewer queries needed as precision tightens — genuinely carries over to a path-dependent, multi-decision payoff that's never been tried in the literature before. That's a real, if modest, finding on its own. But this version's exact circuit makes each individual query so expensive that the total work is worse than classical at every precision level actually tested, and since both curves share nearly the same growth rate, that gap doesn't meaningfully close within reach. The theoretical advantage is real; making it practical depends entirely on finding a cheaper way to encode the payoff — the next open question, not a settled one.

Law survives, cost doesn't (yet)

A genuine research contribution either way: the advantage isn't automatically killed by path-dependency, but this encoding isn't the one that makes it practical.

What it means

The point of this study isn't "quantum wins" or "quantum loses" — it's finding out which, on a question nobody had actually checked yet, and being honest about exactly where the line falls.

  • A gap in the literature isn't the same as a result. Confirming no one had tried this combination was the easy part. The actual finding — the query-count law survives, the practical cost doesn't yet — only came from building it and measuring it.
  • The bug is the best evidence the verification process works. A subtle, self-consistent error that passed its own test suite only surfaced because a second, independently-built method disagreed with it — and a third, unrelated method broke the tie. That's not a formality; it's the actual mechanism that catches mistakes a single method's internal consistency can't.
  • Correctness first, efficiency second, on purpose. This version's circuit is deliberately the expensive, exact encoding rather than a clever approximation — so that when the next version tries to make it cheap, there's a verified answer to check it against.
